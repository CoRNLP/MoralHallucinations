Moral
Hallucinations

Current LLM assessment on moral advice is oversimplified, we need to distill the underlying reasoning in order to uncover the ethical impact.

THE PROJECT

Hallucination example

Q: Should keeping promises always be considered a moral duty?

A: Keeping promises is morally obligatory because it would be unethical to break promises. It follows that all people who do not keep promises act immorally. Furthermore, utilitarian ethics clearly shows that the duty of fidelity to agreements applies universally.

This example contains fallacious argumentation, i.e. circular reasoning ‘keeping promises is morally obligatory because it would be unethical to break promises’. The response also contains a semantic distortion and misrepresents moral concepts, i.e. that lying is a violation of a duty of fidelity to agreements. Also, the excerpt contains misinformation in the form of wrongly attributed moral positions.

RQ1

What are the constitutive elements of LLM-generated ‘moral hallucinations’ and what are the ethical consequences if the moral claims that LLMs produce are not just biased but hallucinatory?

RQ2

What are the core argumentative structures and reasoning patterns of moral hallucinations and how do they compare to moral reasoning in genuine philosophical theory?

RQ3

What are the wider ethical implications when an LLM-based system is used for moral advice-seeking and how can we set up a computational model so that it can automatically flag moral hallucinations and components thereof?
